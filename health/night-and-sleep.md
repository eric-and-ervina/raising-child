He can be warm, safe, and still miserable | One 9-point sweep ([`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §4), then straight to the 5 S's: swaddle → side hold → **shush as loud as he's crying** → small fast jiggle → finger to suck. 3–5 minutes, one change at a time |

1. **A crying baby on his back in an empty cot is safe.** A sleeping baby on your chest on a sofa is not.
2. **He is not manipulating you.** At this age there is no part of his brain that can. He cannot form a habit by being held.
3. **The goal tonight is not "he sleeps through".** It is: he sleeps in safe places, and **each of you gets one solid block of sleep.** That's it. That's a successful night for the next 8 weeks.

## 1. Why the put-down fails — the mechanics (2 minutes, worth knowing)

Knowing *why* stops it feeling personal.

- **He has no idea he is a separate person.** For nine months, "asleep" meant warm, moving, contained, and against a heartbeat. The cot is none of those. This is the "fourth trimester" — not a philosophy, just the state of his nervous system.
- **Newborn sleep cycles are short and start light.** A cycle is roughly **50–60 minutes**, and newborns enter it through **active (light) sleep** — twitching, eye movements, grunting, half-open eyes. A transfer at minute 10 lands right in the window where he wakes easily. **Deep sleep usually arrives after ~20 minutes.**
- **The startle (Moro) reflex** fires when he feels himself drop or when his arms are free — and peaks around **1–2 months**, exactly when the crying peaks. A firm swaddle is the only thing that reliably blunts it.
- **Four things change at once when you lay him down:** temperature (your chest is ~37 °C, the sheet is 26 °C), smell (you disappear), pressure (the firm mattress), and sound (your breathing stops). Any one of those can wake him.
- **He is not being difficult, and you are not creating a bad habit.** "He'll never sleep on his own if you hold him" is false at this age — the evidence on responding to newborn crying is unambiguous, and the parenting guide says the same thing in longer form ([`../parenting/parenting-guide-raising-a-healthy-grounded-boy.md`](../parenting/parenting-guide-raising-a-healthy-grounded-boy.md)).

**What this means in practice:** for the first 8–12 weeks, your job is not to teach him to self-settle. It is to **help him fall asleep** and then **keep him asleep through the transfer**. Independent settling is a 3–4 month skill, not a newborn one.

## 2. Getting him into the cot

### 2.1 The 7-step lay-down (use this every time)

The mistake everyone makes is putting him down **when his eyes close**. Eyes closing is stage one. You want the limp arm.

1. **Get him properly asleep first.** Hold him chest-to-chest, swaddled, with steady shush and a small jiggle. Watch for: **eyes fully closed and still, breathing slow and regular, face slack, and — the test — his arm hanging limp when you lift it and letting it drop.** Usually **15–20 minutes** from the moment he goes quiet. Rushing this step is the single biggest cause of "he cries when I put him down".
2. **Warm the cot before you cross the room.** A heat pack, a hot water bottle (removed), or a folded towel you've been sitting on, on the sheet for 2 minutes, then taken away. **Never leave a heat source in the cot and never put him on anything warm to the touch** — it should feel neutral-warm, not hot; test with your wrist.
3. **Change the sound before you move, not after.** Keep the shush/white noise running and *continuous* — do not let it stop at the cot. A sudden silence is a wake-up signal. White noise ≤50 dB, 1–2 m away, per [`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §5.4.
4. **Lay him on his side first, then roll him onto his back.** Bottom → back → head, exactly as in [`care-techniques.md`](care-techniques.md) §1. Never let his head drop or his arms fly free mid-move.
5. **Keep contact for 30 seconds after he's down.** Both hands on him — one on his chest, one on his head — and hold **completely still**. Do not rub or pat yet. Stillness plus pressure is what replaces the feeling of being held.
6. **Withdraw in stages.** Hands off the head first, then the chest, keeping one hand on his belly for another 30 seconds, then take that away. **Count to 60 before you step back from the cot.** Each stage, he may stir — freeze, wait 10 seconds, he usually settles.
7. **Leave the room before he's in deep sleep, not after.** If you stand over the cot watching, you'll intervene at every normal twitch — and newborn sleep is *full* of twitches, grunts, and 30-second startles that are not waking.

**If he wakes within 5 minutes:** pick him up and start again from step 1. Do not stand over the cot shushing an escalating baby for 20 minutes — that's harder on everyone. **Two failed transfers in a row means the sleep pressure is gone**; feed him if due, or take a 20-minute tag-team break, then retry.

### 2.2 The cot ladder — five rungs, no crying it out

When he will not stay down, work **down** this ladder, in this order. Each rung is "less of you". You are not leaving him to cry; you are giving him the smallest amount of help that works, then a little less next time.

| Rung | What it is | Do it for | Then |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5** | Fully in arms, chest-to-chest, swaddled, moving | Until limp (15–20 min) | Go to rung 4 |
| **4** | In arms, **still** — no walking, no bounce, just held and shushed | 2–3 min | Lay down (7-step), hands on, → rung 3 |
| **3** | In the cot, **your hands on him** — one on chest, one on head, totally still | 1–2 min after he settles | Withdraw in stages |
| **2** | In the cot, **one hand on his chest or belly**, firm and still. Add a slow "shhh" only if he fusses | Up to 2 min | Take the hand away when his breathing slows |
| **1** | In the cot, **hand hovering** or just your voice from beside the cot | 30–60 s | Step back, stay in the room |
| **0** | Out of the room, door ajar, noise on | — | If he cries properly (not a twitch), go back to the rung **above** where he last settled — not to the top |

**The two rules of the ladder:** go **down** one rung at a time but **up** as many as needed; and **never skip from rung 5 to rung 0** because you're desperate — that's crying it out, and it isn't for a newborn.

**A grunt is not a cry.** Newborns are loud sleepers: they grunt, strain, squeak, and startle with their eyes half open and go straight back under. **Wait 20 seconds before you touch him.** More sleep is lost to parents rescuing a sleeping baby than to the baby waking.

### 2.3 The feed question at night — cue or clock?

Night feeds are non-negotiable at this age: **8–12 feeds in 24 hours, including overnight**, and **wake him every 2–3 hours** until the PD says his weight gain allows longer stretches.

But "he's crying at night" is not automatically "he's hungry":

| What you see | Feed? |
|---|---|
| Rooting, turning his head with an open mouth, hand to mouth, sucking noises, stirring | **Yes — feed now**, before he escalates |
| Fed well **<90 minutes ago**, and now **arching, turning his head away, pushing off the breast/bottle** | **No.** That's gas, overstimulation, or overtired. Feeding adds gas and makes it worse |
| Fed **2–3 hours ago** and crying | **Yes** — this is a normal night gap |
| Fed 30 minutes ago, wants to suck, no rooting, calm-ish | **Suck, not feed** — clean finger or pacifier if you're using one. Non-nutritive sucking is real soothing, not a trick |
| Crying **20–40 minutes after** a feed with back-arching and spit-up | Feed normally, but keep him upright 20–30 min and mention reflux to the PD ([`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §9.1) |

**The overfeeding trap at night:** a tired parent's instinct is "he's crying, give the breast/bottle again". If that turns into 5 feeds in 2 hours, he gets a stretched stomach, more gas, more spit-up, and *more* crying — and you get a baby who genuinely can't settle. **Two feeds within an hour is a signal to look for another cause, not to feed a third time.**

### 2.4 The night change — don't wake him for it

- **Change him before the feed if he's dirty; after the feed if he's only wet and asleep.** A wet nappy alone is not worth a wake window at 3 a.m.
- Do it **in the cot or on a mat on the floor** with the light dim, no talking, no eye contact, no unswaddling more than the bottom half.
- Keep a **cloth over his front** — the cool air triggers a pee every time.
- If a change reliably wakes him fully, change him **mid-feed** (after the first side), when he's already roused, rather than at the end.

## 3. The night itself — the protocol

### 3.1 The shape of a normal night (so you stop being surprised)

| Time | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| **19:00–23:00** | The hardest window of the day. Cluster feeding, evening fussiness, the 5–11 p.m. peak ([`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §7). **He may feed every 30–60 minutes and be impossible to put down.** This is normal and it is the *reason* the roster puts two adults on it |
| **23:00–02:00** | Usually the best stretch — 2–3 hours if you're lucky. This is the block to protect |
| **02:00–05:00** | The second feed, and often the fussiest one. **Nobody's judgment is good here.** Follow the protocol, don't invent a new plan |
| **05:00–07:00** | Many newborns are wide awake and grunty at dawn. Hand him to the parent who slept at 2 and go back to bed |

**He will not sleep more than 3–4 hours in one stretch at this age**, and that is not a problem to fix. A 3-hour stretch at 4 weeks is genuinely good.

### 3.2 The night-feed script (4 steps, in the dark)

Say it out loud once in daylight so neither of you has to think at 2 a.m.:

1. **Whoever is on duty hears him, checks the clock, and gets him.** The other parent does not move. Not to help, not to watch, not to comment.
2. **Feed:** dim light only (a warm nightlight, not the ceiling light), no talking above a whisper, no play, no phone screen in his face. Ervina feeds; if it's a bottle night, the on-duty parent feeds.
3. **Burp, change, resettle** — the on-duty parent does all three, so Ervina's awake stretch is as short as possible ([`../food-and-feeding/breastfeeding.md`](../food-and-feeding/breastfeeding.md) §7).
4. **Back to the cot, lights off, done.** Log the feed and the nappy in one line. Then the on-duty parent sleeps — immediately, without "just checking" anything on the phone.

**The 2-hour rule:** if he hasn't resettled **2 hours** after a feed, the on-duty parent **wakes the other one**. Not to fix the baby — to take a 20-minute block so the first parent can lie down. Marathons alone at night are how resentment and intrusive thoughts start ([`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §13).

### 3.3 The three night rules

1. **Only one of you is awake at a time.** "Both awake, both exhausted" is the worst possible configuration. If your presence isn't required, you are asleep — earplugs in, other room if you have one.
2. **No decisions, no arguments, no research after 23:00.** Anything that needs deciding gets written on the fridge and decided at 10 a.m. with coffee. This is a rule because 3 a.m. judgment is demonstrably bad, not because either of you is unreasonable.
3. **Whoever is on duty owns the resettle.** The other one doesn't appear in the doorway at minute 40 with a suggestion.

### 3.4 The dawn handover

The 05:00–08:00 block is the one that decides how the day goes. The parent who slept through the night's second half takes him — **walk, sling, or hold him** — while the other sleeps until 08:00 with the door shut. This is a task, not babysitting, and it is what makes the rest of the roster survive the week.

## 4. "He will only sleep on a person" — the safe options, ranked

This is the honest core of the problem: many newborns **will not** sleep in a cot for the first weeks, and no amount of technique changes that on a bad night. So the question becomes *where is it safe for him to sleep on a person*, and the answer is narrow.

| Situation | Verdict | Why / rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Awake adult, baby in a sling/carrier, you're walking or standing** | ✅ **Safe, and the best tool you have** | TICKS fit ([`care-techniques.md`](care-techniques.md) §11), face always visible, chin off chest, no cooking/hot drinks/stairs, check him every few minutes. This is how you survive 05:00–08:00 |
| **Awake adult, baby asleep in your arms on a chair, and you are genuinely awake** | ⚠️ Acceptable for short stretches | Only if you are truly alert. Not on a sofa. Not with a blanket near his face. **The moment you feel drowsy, he goes to the cot** |
| **Baby asleep on your chest overnight, you asleep too** | ❌ **No** | This is the classic SIDS/overlay death scenario — and it happens to parents who "never thought they'd fall asleep" |
| **Either of you asleep with him on a sofa, armchair, or beanbag** | ❌ **Never, not for a minute** | The highest-risk surface there is — many times more dangerous than a bed, because you can't roll away and he can slump into the gap |
| **Bed-sharing in your bed** | ❌ **Not in this house** | AAP and Singapore MOH both say no. Soft mattress, duvets, pillows, an adult body, and a 3-week-old do not mix. **The cot next to your bed is the plan** |
| **Car seat, swing, rocker, bouncer, nest, or a "lounger" as a bed** | ❌ **Never** | Positional asphyxia — his chin drops to his chest and his airway closes silently. Awake and supervised only |
| **Pram, flat, next to you while you're awake** | ✅ Fine | Not a substitute for the overnight cot |

### 4.1 The 3 a.m. doze protocol (the one that matters most)

You will feel yourself going under while holding him. It is not a character flaw — it's biology at 3 a.m. on 90 minutes of sleep. **Have a plan, not a hope:**

1. **Notice the signs:** heavy eyelids, the "nod", losing your place, a warm heavy feeling in your arms, or the thought "just five minutes".
2. **Say it out loud:** "I'm falling asleep." Saying it to the room somehow makes it manageable, and it wakes the other parent.
3. **Stand up.** Literally. Sitting in a dark room with a warm baby is the trap; standing and walking breaks it.
4. **If you can't stand safely:** **put him in the cot, on his back, and walk away.** He can cry for ten minutes in a cot. He cannot be safely held by someone who is asleep.
5. **If the other parent is asleep — wake them.** This is exactly what the 2-hour rule and the tag-team are for. Waking them at 3 a.m. once is better than the alternative.
6. **If you are alone in the flat with him:** cot, door open, set a **10-minute timer**, lie on the floor next to the cot (not in the bed with him), and repeat. Do not sit in an armchair with him.

### 4.2 If you wake up and he was in your arms

This happens to most parents at least once. What to do, in order — no shame spiral:

1. **Check him now:** breathing normally, pink, warm, waking normally? Then he's fine. Wake him gently if you're unsure.
2. **Note the circumstances:** which surface, were you on a sofa, were there pillows/duvets, how long.
3. **Change the setup tonight, not tomorrow.** Options: the cot within arm's reach of the bed (it already is), a floor mat beside the cot for the on-duty parent, a chair **not** in the dark corner, the sling for the 5 a.m. shift, waking the other parent earlier.
4. **Tell each other.** Not as a confession — as data. A near-miss that gets repeated silently is the actual risk.
5. **Never** "just this once, it was fine last time". That's how the once becomes the pattern.

## 5. Your own sleep — the maths that makes this survivable

The rule in the program is Ervina's **4 uninterrupted hours in every 24** ([`first-six-weeks-program.md`](first-six-weeks-program.md) §3, [`postpartum-recovery.md`](postpartum-recovery.md)). Eric needs a block too — a parent who has slept 3 hours total is a safety problem, not a hero.

- **Blocks beat totals.** 5 hours in two chunks is worth far more than 5 hours in 40-minute pieces. **Protect two blocks of 3+ hours** rather than chasing an 8-hour night that isn't coming.
- **Sleep in 90-minute units** where you can — close to one full cycle, so you wake less groggy. A 3-hour block is two cycles.
- **"Off duty" means:** earplugs or noise-reducing buds in, in another room if the flat allows, door shut, and **no chores**. Dishes are not rest.
- **The on-duty parent naps in 20 minutes, not 90,** during the day — a 90-minute nap in the late afternoon wrecks the night shift.
- **Caffeine:** fine, but **none after 2 p.m.** if you're on the night shift. It has a 5–6 hour half-life and you need to be able to sleep at 9 a.m.
- **Eat before 7 p.m.**, both of you. Hungry parents at 9 p.m. make bad calls ([`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §7.2).
- **The two-week test:** if either of you has had **fewer than 4 hours of unbroken sleep in 3 days**, the roster changes **today** — call in grandparents, pay for help, take leave. That's a tripwire, not a preference.
- **Don't drive on it.** Drowsy driving after a broken night is comparable to drink-driving. If you're that tired, Grab it.

**If the broken nights are stacking into something worse** — can't sleep even when he sleeps, can't eat, crying daily, feeling nothing, or thoughts that scare you — that's a medical symptom, not a sleep problem. [`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §13.4 and [`postpartum-recovery.md`](postpartum-recovery.md) §6 have the screening and the numbers (SOS **1-767**, IMH **6389 2222**).

## 6. Day sleep — getting one cot nap in

You do not need to fix night sleep by fixing day sleep, but one cot nap a day is what teaches him the cot is a normal place to be — and it's the only way you get a shower.

1. **Watch the wake window, not the clock.** A newborn is awake for only **45–60 minutes** including the feed. Past that, cortisol is up and he's much harder to settle ([`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §4.6). **Start the settle at the first yawn**, not at the first scream.
2. **Pick one nap a day** — usually the late-morning one — to be a cot nap. The rest can be in arms, in the sling, or on you. This is deliberate, not lazy: one hard thing at a time.
3. **Do the same 4 things every time:** dim the room, swaddle, white noise, the same 30-second still hold. Babies this age learn **sequences**, not instructions.
4. **If it fails twice, stop and take the sling nap.** A cot nap that costs 40 minutes of crying is a net loss for both of you today. Try again tomorrow.
5. **Do not keep him awake to "tire him out".** An overtired newborn sleeps *worse*. This is the single most common self-inflicted sleep problem in the first three months.
6. **Bright, noisy, chatty days; dark, dull, boring nights.** Daylight on his face in the morning and no play at 3 a.m. is the whole day/night training, and it takes weeks, not days.

## 7. When it isn't just normal — the two-week escalation

Most of this is developmental and resolves. Some of it is a treatable problem wearing the same costume. **If the nights have not improved at all in two weeks, or are getting worse, work this list and then book the PD.**

| Check | What it looks like at night | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| **Reflux** | Cries **20–40 min after** feeds, arches, spits up, worse lying flat, better upright, hiccups, hoarse cry | [`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §9.1 |
| **CMPA (milk protein allergy)** | Persistent crying with feeds, mucus/blood in stool, eczema, poor gain | crying §9.3 — **PD, not self-diagnosis** |
| **Overtiredness** | Screams harder the more you rock; settles only after a huge effort; short frantic naps | crying §4.6 |
| **Too hot** | Sweaty neck, flushed, restless, wakes every 30 min. Singapore flats + aircon off = the commonest miss | [`newborn-care.md`](newborn-care.md) — 24–26 °C, one layer, no hat |
| **Hunger / poor milk transfer** | Wakes every 45–60 min, feeds for an hour, still hungry, fewer than 6 wet nappies, not back to birth weight | [`../food-and-feeding/breastfeeding.md`](../food-and-feeding/breastfeeding.md) §4, [`weight-and-growth.md`](weight-and-growth.md) |
| **Pain** | A cry that only happens in one position, or on one movement | crying §10 — the head-to-toe pain hunt |
| **Nasal blockage** | Can't feed and breathe at once, pulls off, snuffly, worse lying flat | [`care-techniques.md`](care-techniques.md) §6 for the technique, [`illness-care.md`](illness-care.md) §3 for the sick-day routine |
| **A parent who is unwell** | Not the baby at all — postnatal depression/anxiety, or sheer exhaustion | crying §13.4 + [`postpartum-recovery.md`](postpartum-recovery.md) §6 |

**What to say when you call the PD** (adapt §15 of the crying file): "He's 4 weeks, 4.2 kg. He wakes every 45 minutes at night, feeds 10 times a day, has 8 wet nappies, and takes 40 minutes to resettle each time. He arches after feeds and spits up. We've tried swaddling, white noise, upright holding, and a consistent cot routine. Is this reflux, a feeding problem, or normal? What would you like us to try, and what would make you want to see him?" — **bring the log.**

## 8. Never do these to get sleep

| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| **Let a newborn cry it out** | Not developmentally appropriate under ~4–6 months, and never for a hungry or unwell baby. It doesn't teach sleep, it teaches him no one is coming |
| **Sleep with him on your chest, or in bed with you, "just tonight"** | The single most common setup for a sleep-related death in the first months |
| **Put him to sleep on his side or tummy "because he sleeps better"** | He might. It's also the strongest modifiable SIDS risk there is |
| **Prop the mattress, use a nest/positioner/lounger, or let him sleep in the car seat or swing** | Positional asphyxia — silent, and it happens in minutes |
| **Swaddle with his arms pinned so tight he can't move, or past the first roll** | Hip and shoulder risk; and a rolling baby needs free arms |
| **Add cereal to a bottle to make him sleep** | Choking, no benefit, and it does not work |
| **Give gripe water, "wind oil", herbal drops, or anything sedating** | No evidence, real harm; several traditional remedies are dangerous to newborns |
| **Leave him on a changing table, bed, or sofa "for a second"** | The one-second falls are the classic first injury |
| **Trade sleep by having one parent drive him around at night** | Drowsy driving plus a car seat is two risks at once |
| **Shake, shout at, or handle him roughly because you're exhausted** | Cot, on his back, walk away — every time ([`crying-and-soothing.md`](crying-and-soothing.md) §13.2) |

## 9. What changes, and when (so you know this ends)

| Age | Sleep reality | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| **Weeks 1–3** | 14–17 h/24 h in 2–4 h fragments, no day/night sense. Will only sleep on a person for many babies | This file as written. Assist every sleep. Nobody trains anything |
| **Weeks 4–8** | **The peak** — evening cluster, hardest to settle, shortest stretches. Also the peak of parental desperation | Roster discipline. Tag-team at 20 min. Do not start "sleep training" during the peak |
| **Weeks 8–12** | Stretches lengthen (4–5 h for some), settling gets easier, cry curve falls | Begin **drowsy-but-awake** practice for *some* naps — put him down before he's fully out, and accept it failing |
| **3–4 months** | Smoother cycles, longer night stretches, the **4-month sleep regression** (a real developmental shift in sleep architecture) | Swaddle **out** at the first roll → sleep sack. A consistent bedtime routine. Now gentle settling methods and a bedtime ladder are reasonable — with the PD, never for hunger or illness |
| **6 months+** | May still wake for feeds; cot can move toward his own room | Sleep-work methods have evidence behind them now. Different file |

**The honest timeline:** for most babies this is **hard for 6–8 weeks and noticeably better by 3–4 months.** Some are harder for longer. If you are at week 3 and it feels impossible — that is the expected report, not a verdict on you.

## 13. Sources and honesty notes

- **Newborn sleep architecture:** term newborns sleep 14–17 h/24 h in short cycles (~50–60 min) and enter sleep through active sleep, which is why transfers fail early — AAP *Healthy Sleep Habits* and infant sleep research (Anders, Coons & Guilleminault) are the basis for the 15–20 minute transfer guidance here.
- **Safe sleep:** the "alone, on the back, in a cot, in the parents' room" framing follows **AAP** and **Singapore MOH** guidance; the "no sofa/armchair, ever" line reflects the consistent finding that sofa-sharing carries by far the highest risk in sleep-related infant deaths.
- **Responding to crying / "spoiling":** the evidence does not support the idea that responding to a newborn creates dependence; the parenting guide (§attachment) covers the longer version.
- **Extinction/"cry it out":** has evidence from ~4–6 months; there is no good case for it in a 3-week-old. This file deliberately does not use it.
- **"The limp arm" and the 20-minute window** are practical rules of thumb, not measurements — they are widely used by nursery nurses and they work, but every baby differs.
- **This is general information.** It cannot see Eldric. If your instinct says something is wrong, call the PD or go in — that outranks every table here.
