## 1. The first two weeks, day by day

| When | What's normal | What's not — call |
|---|---|---|
| **First 24h** | Sore, shaky, exhausted, hungry or not hungry at all. Bleeding like a heavy period. Afterpains when feeding. Swollen, numb perineum. | Soaking a pad in an hour, dizziness on standing past the first time you get up, feeling faint |
| **Day 2–3** | **Milk comes in** — breasts hard, hot, heavy (see [`../food-and-feeding/breastfeeding.md`](../food-and-feeding/breastfeeding.md) §5). Bleeding still red, still fairly heavy. Sweating at night. Cramps while feeding. **This is usually the hardest day, emotionally as well as physically.** | Fever ≥38 °C, a red hot wedge on one breast with flu symptoms, no urine passed within ~6h of a birth, worsening rather than easing pain |
| **Day 4–7** | Bleeding lightens to pink/brown. Afterpains ease. Stitches pull when sitting. First proper bowel movement — often the thing she dreads most. | Foul-smelling discharge, the stitch opening or new severe pain, calf pain or swelling in one leg |
| **Week 2** | Bleeding becomes a brownish-yellow discharge. Energy flickers back in short bursts. Mood settles. Wound discomfort mostly gone. | Bleeding going **back to bright red and heavy** after it had settled, fever, feeling persistently low or hopeless (see §6) |
| **Week 3–6** | Weeping pale yellow/white. Occasional twinges. Swollen feet and hands resolve. | Anything in the red-flags list, at any point |

**Lochia, in plain numbers:** bright red for about 3–5 days → brownish-pink for about 1–2 weeks → yellowish-white up to about 4–6 weeks. It should get steadily *lighter*, not heavier. **A pad soaked in an hour, or clots bigger than a golf ball, is an emergency** — 995, do not drive her yourself.

## 2. The stitch — perineal care that actually helps

One stitch is minor; it still hurts, and it still needs a routine for a week or two.

- **Keep it clean and dry.** Shower daily, rinse with plain warm water, pat dry front to back (never rub). Change pads every 2–4 hours, and whenever wet.
- **Cold then warm.** Cold packs (wrapped in a cloth, never directly on skin) for the first 24–48 hours to reduce swelling; warm sitz baths or a warm shower after that for comfort.
- **Sit smart.** Sitting on a hard chair puts all the pressure on the stitch. Lie down more than you sit; when you do sit, perch slightly to one side, use a soft cushion, or sit on a feeding pillow. **Lying down beats sitting down, every time, for the first week.**
- **Pain relief:** paracetamol, and ibuprofen if her doctor says it is fine — both are generally compatible with breastfeeding. Confirm the dose with the doctor or pharmacist. **Do not** tough it out to avoid tablets; unmanaged pain makes feeding and sleep worse, and both of those are medical inputs.
- **Poo without fear.** Constipation is normal (painkillers, dehydration, fear), and straining is what actually threatens the stitch. Fluids, fibre, and a stool softener if her doctor agrees. Support the perineum with a clean pad and don't hold your breath. A first bowel movement is allowed to be frightening and still be fine.
- **Tell the doctor** at the 6-week check if she still has pain, a dragging or heavy feeling, or anything that feels "not right" down there. That is common and treatable — pelvic floor physiotherapy exists in Singapore.

## 3. The unglamorous list (all normal, all worth saying out loud)

- **Night sweats** for a week or two — the body shedding the pregnancy's extra fluid.
- **Swollen feet and hands** that get *worse* around day 3–5 before getting better.
- **Hair loss** starting about 3–4 months after birth — dramatic, temporary, and not a sign of anything wrong.
- **Skin changes, stretch marks, a softer belly.** The uterus takes about 6 weeks to shrink back; the belly takes longer.
- **Haemorrhoids** and a sore bottom — common after a vaginal birth.
- **Breast leaking** at unpredictable moments, including when she hears a baby cry (any baby).
- **Appetite swings,** and thirst that is genuinely enormous if breastfeeding.
- **Feeling emotional in waves** — see §6, which is not a throwaway section.

## 4. Feeding her body

- **Hydration is the big one.** Water within reach at every feed, and more than she thinks she needs. A full glass before and after each feed is a workable rule.
- **Eat properly, and often.** Breastfeeding costs energy; skipping meals to "rest" backfires. The confinement cooking plan covers this — Eric's job is that the food appears without her having to plan, shop, or ask.
- **Iron, calcium, and protein** matter while healing and feeding — follow the gynae's advice on supplements.
- **Caffeine** is fine in modest amounts; watch the total, and watch it more after 2 p.m.
- **Alcohol** — best avoided while breastfeeding, and never near a night feed. If she does drink, wait a couple of hours before feeding.
- **The thing to protect above all** is the long block of sleep in §5, not the diet.

## 5. Sleep and the shift system — the actual medical intervention

Sleep deprivation is not a badge; it is a direct risk factor for low mood, poor healing, and mistakes at 3 a.m.

- **Protect one 4-hour stretch of sleep in every 24.** Four uninterrupted hours is the target that changes how a person feels. It can be Eric, grandparents, or a helper who covers it — the point is that it happens daily and is planned, not hoped for.
- **Tag-team in blocks.** One parent on duty, the other genuinely off (earplugs, another room) rather than "sort of awake". Twenty-minute blocks work when nobody will take a whole shift.
- **Eric's night job:** get up for the burp, change, and resettle after each feed, and handle any bottle night ([`../food-and-feeding/formula-and-bottles.md`](../food-and-feeding/formula-and-bottles.md) §9) so her awake window per feed is as short as possible.
- **"Sleep when the baby sleeps"** is good advice delivered badly. A better version: **whichever parent is off-duty does not do chores instead** — rest is the task.
- **If it all breaks down** — she is awake all night with a crying baby and a sleeping partner — that is the moment to call in the grandparents or spend money on help. It is a resource problem, not a willpower problem.

### 5.1 Why four hours, and not "rest"

Sleep after birth is a different problem from ordinary tiredness, and it deserves its own section because it is **one of the most modifiable risk factors for postpartum depression** — and because it is a physical-safety issue in a flat with a newborn.

- **Fragmented sleep is not the same as less sleep.** A person woken every 45 minutes can spend eight hours in bed and still get almost no **deep sleep**, because deep sleep needs an uninterrupted stretch to arrive. That's why "I was in bed for six hours" and "I got six hours" feel different — and why **one 4-hour block beats eight broken hours**, every time.
- **Sleep debt is cumulative and it does not announce itself.** It shows up first as **irritability and a flat mood**, then as poor memory and word-finding, lowered pain tolerance (her stitch will hurt more), worse healing, and a shorter fuse with everyone. By the time she "feels terrible", she is several nights deep.
- **It affects feeding indirectly.** Sleep loss does not usually reduce milk supply — supply is driven by removal — but it makes **let-down harder and feeds feel worse**, which is often read as "my supply is dropping". It usually isn't.
- **It is a safety issue.** Falling asleep while feeding on a chair or sofa with a newborn is one of the highest-risk scenarios in [`night-and-sleep.md`](night-and-sleep.md) §4. **A mother who keeps dozing off with him is not careless — she is sleep-deprived, and the fix is the roster, not a warning.**
- **She will under-report it.** "I'm fine" is the default answer from someone whose job it now is to keep a baby alive. **Eric's job is to count her sleep, not ask about it.**

### 5.2 The triage ladder

**Tonight** — when she has had under 4 hours unbroken for two nights running:

1. **She goes to bed and the door shuts.** Not "resting on the sofa where she can hear him". Earplugs, or the far room, or a fan for white noise.
2. **Eric owns the whole cycle, not the burp.** If she is breastfeeding, he brings the baby to her **only for the feed** and takes him back the moment she's done — she does not get up, does not change him, does not resettle him. If a bottle is established (week 3+), **Eric takes one entire feed** and she sleeps through it ([`first-six-weeks-program.md`](first-six-weeks-program.md) §3, Phase B).
3. **The monitor goes with the parent who is on duty.** If Eric has the baby, the baby monitor is not in her room. Listening for him is the thing that stops her sleeping.
4. **One block, not three naps.** Four uninterrupted hours. A nap on the sofa while the baby naps does not substitute.

**This week:**

- **Rewrite the roster so that her block is a fixed time**, not "whenever" ([`first-six-weeks-program.md`](first-six-weeks-program.md) §3).
- **Bring in a third adult for one block a day** — grandparent, helper, a friend on a rota. The 09:00–13:00 block is the easiest to hand over.
- **Move the bottle forward** if it was planned for later. One bottle a day at weeks 3–4 buys her a real 4–5 hour stretch ([`../food-and-feeding/formula-and-bottles.md`](../food-and-feeding/formula-and-bottles.md) §6).
- **Decide what gets dropped.** In a 689 sqft flat with a newborn, something goes: the tidiness standard, the cooking from scratch, the visit you said yes to. **Choose it on purpose so it isn't chosen by exhaustion.**

**This month:**

- If the 4-hour block has not happened on most days, **the plan has failed, not her.** That's the tripwire in [`first-six-weeks-program.md`](first-six-weeks-program.md) §10: change the roster, call in help, or spend money on it.

### 5.3 What does not count as rest

- **Chores with the baby asleep** — laundry is not sleep, and "I'll just do one load" is how a block disappears.
- **Scrolling in bed.** It delays sleep onset and, at 3 a.m., it fills the gap where sleep should be with other people's opinions about babies.
- **"Sleep when the baby sleeps"** delivered as an instruction. It works when someone else is holding the baby; it is close to useless when she is the one listening for him.
- **Hosting.** A visitor during her sleep window is not company, it's work (§9).
- **Caffeine after 2 p.m.** Half-life of 5–6 hours: a 4 p.m. coffee is still a quarter in her system at 2 a.m.

### 5.4 When sleep debt has become a medical problem

The line between "shattered" and "unwell" is not how tired she feels. It's these:

| What you notice | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Can't sleep even when the baby is asleep and someone else has him** — for 2+ weeks | Insomnia is a core symptom of PPD, not a cause of it | **Ask Dr Paul for the EPDS (§6) and say this sentence out loud** |
| **Falling asleep without warning** — mid-sentence, mid-feed, standing | Severe sleep debt or an underlying condition | **Restructure tonight** (§5.2), and tell the PD or her GP this week |
| **Confusion, not sleeping at all, hearing or seeing things, strange beliefs** | Postpartum psychosis — an emergency | **995 now** (§6) |
| **Thoughts of harming herself or the baby** | An emergency, regardless of how calm she seems | **SOS 1-767 / IMH 6389 2222 / 995** — now, not tomorrow |
| **Dreading the nights, panic at 6 p.m.** | Anticipatory anxiety, very common and treatable | Tell the PD; it responds to support and treatment, and it does not mean she's failing |

**The one line for Eric:** when she says "I'm fine", the correct response is not to argue. It's *"I've got him from ten to two, go and shut the door"* — and then to actually do it, including getting up, including the nappy, including the resettle.

## 6. Her mental health — the part that is easiest to miss

**Baby blues** are the baseline, not a warning sign: up to 8 in 10 mothers get tearful, irritable, and overwhelmed, typically peaking around **day 3–5** and settling by about **2 weeks**. They need rest, food, and company — not treatment.

**Postpartum depression (PPD)** is different, and it is common enough that you should expect to look for it rather than be surprised by it. Warning signs, roughly any time in the first year:

- Low, flat, or tearful most of the day, most days, **lasting more than 2 weeks** and not lifting with rest.
- Losing interest in things she normally enjoys, including the baby.
- Sleeping badly *even when the baby is asleep*, or sleeping far too much.
- Anxiety that will not switch off — intrusive thoughts, dread, checking the baby repeatedly, being unable to leave him with anyone, not even Eric.
- Feeling like a bad mother, hopeless, guilty, or like the family would be better off without her.
- Appetite gone, or eating for comfort with no relief.
- **Snapping at, or withdrawing from, Eric and everyone else.**

**Postpartum psychosis** is rare but an emergency: confusion, not sleeping at all, hearing or seeing things, strange or grandiose beliefs, or behaviour that is out of character and frightening. **Call 995 now** — do not wait for the 6-week check.

**What to do:**

- **Screen it, don't guess it.** The **Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)** is 10 short questions and is the standard screen; it is normally done at the postnatal check and can be repeated. A score of **10 or more** means "talk to a doctor now", and **any thought of self-harm is urgent regardless of the score**. Ask Dr Paul for it at the 6-week visit — and sooner if she seems off.
- **Anyone can raise it.** Eric's job is to say out loud, gently and non-accusatorily: *"You've not been yourself for two weeks and I think it's worth talking to someone — it's common, and it's treatable."* Do not make it about the baby or about her "coping".
- **Where to get help in Singapore:**
  - **SOS (24h): 1-767** or WhatsApp **9151 1767** — immediate emotional support.
  - **IMH Mental Health Helpline (24h): 6389 2222**.
  - **Her gynae / a GP** — the first, fastest door; PPD is a medical condition, not a character flaw.
  - **KKH and NUH** run perinatal mental health services; her doctor can refer.
  - **995** for anything in the psychosis list above.
- **Eric's mental health counts too.** Paternal depression is real, common, and worsened by sleep loss and a sense of uselessness. If he is flat, angry, or numb for weeks, the same doors apply.

> **The one line to remember:** the tearfulness of day 5 is normal; the flatness that is still there at week 5 is not something to wait out.

## 7. Pelvic floor and core — the 6-week gate

- **Weeks 1–6:** gentle **pelvic floor exercises** (they help with leaking, heaviness, and healing) and short walks. Start slowly, stop if anything hurts.
- **No lifting heavier than the baby** (plus car seat and pram, realistically) and **no running, jumping, or sit-ups** during this window.
- **Avoid straining on the toilet** — see §2.
- **The 6-week postnatal check is the gate.** Everything beyond it — core work, running, weights — waits for the all-clear. If there is leaking, a heavy dragging feeling, or pain, ask for a **pelvic floor physiotherapy referral**; this is common and fixable, and "it's normal after birth" is not a treatment plan.
- **Diastasis** (a gap between the abdominal muscles) is common and usually closes with time and correct exercise — not crunches.

## 9. Visitors and help — "you are not hosting"

- **She is recovering from a physical event, not putting on a lunch.** Short visits, and no visitor who arrives expecting to be fed, entertained, or passed the baby while she plays host.
- **Nobody with a cold, cough, fever, or recent illness holds Eldric.** Hands washed, first. This includes grandparents — see the visitor rules in [`newborn-care.md`](newborn-care.md).
- **Help means a task, not a visit.** The genuinely useful offers: cook, do laundry, hold the baby while she sleeps, clean the bathroom, take the 4 a.m. shift. Steer every willing visitor to one of those.
- **She can say no.** "Tomorrow doesn't work, come Saturday" is a complete sentence, and Eric's job is to be the one who says it.
- **Protect the feeding and sleeping windows.** Visitors are best outside them, and a "no" during a cluster-feed hour costs nothing.
