## 1. The first hour (skin-to-skin)

- **Skin-to-skin on Ervina's chest** immediately after birth — warmth, first feed, bonding, and better milk let-down. It counts for Eric too, later, at any time.
- **APGAR** at 1 and 5 minutes — a quick score (heart rate, breathing, tone, reflex, colour) out of 10. **A 7–8 at 1 minute is completely ordinary**; it is a snapshot, not a verdict, and it is not a measure of future intelligence or health.
- **Weight, length, head circumference** — 3,590 g / 52 cm / 35 cm for Eldric.
- **Vitamin K injection** and his **ID bands** (matching Ervina's), plus a security tag at some hospitals.
- **First feed within the first hour** if he was willing — see [`../food-and-feeding/breastfeeding.md`](../food-and-feeding/breastfeeding.md).

## 2. Procedures before discharge — the checklist

Singapore hospitals routinely offer these; ask the ward nurse what was actually given, and write it in §7.

| Procedure | When | Why | What to know |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin K injection** | Shortly after birth | Prevents vitamin K deficiency bleeding — a rare but catastrophic bleed into the brain or gut in the first weeks | Offered to **every** newborn. The injection is the standard and reliable route. If you would rather not, ask the PD about the **oral** alternative — it needs a repeated course and is less effective. Do not simply decline without asking |
| **BCG** (tuberculosis) | At birth | Singapore gives BCG at birth (unlike many countries) | Leaves a small spot/pustule that scabs over weeks. If he has redness and swelling with pus at ~6 weeks, that is the expected reaction — mention it at the PD visit rather than panicking |
| **Hepatitis B — dose 1** | At birth | First of three | Dose 2 at ~1 month, dose 3 within the 6-in-1 at ~6 months |
| **Newborn screening (blood spot)** | Ideally **24–72 hours**, before discharge | Screens for treatable conditions that show no symptoms yet | See §3. **Ask when the result arrives** — usually in 1–2 weeks, to you or the PD |
| **Hearing screening** | Before discharge, once settled | Detects hearing loss early | See §4 |
| **Jaundice check (bilirubin)** | Before discharge, and the Day 3–5 visit | Jaundice is nearly universal and mostly harmless — but high levels need treatment | See §5 |
| **Weight at discharge** | Before you leave | The baseline the Day 3–5 check compares against | Record it **with the percent loss from birth weight** |
| **Feed at discharge** | Before you leave | Confirm the feeding plan in writing | Ask the lactation consultant to observe a full feed; see the breastfeeding file §2 |
| **Newborn physical exam** | Before discharge | Heart, hips, spine, genitals, palate, reflexes | Ask them to **walk you through the findings** and note anything flagged for follow-up |

## 3. Newborn screening — the blood spot (heel prick)

A few drops of blood from his heel, taken 24–72 hours after birth, on a card sent to the national screening lab. It is a **screen, not a diagnosis**: a flagged result means "test again", not "he is ill".

It looks for conditions where early treatment changes everything:

- **Congenital hypothyroidism** — treatable, and untreated it causes developmental delay. This is the reason the test is not optional.
- **G6PD deficiency** — see below; genuinely relevant for this family.
- **Congenital adrenal hyperplasia** — a hormone condition needing prompt management.
- **A panel of metabolic disorders** on the expanded programme (amino acid, fatty acid and organic acid disorders), plus others depending on the current national panel. Ask the PD which panel his hospital runs.

> ### The G6PD note — read this one properly
> **G6PD deficiency is common in Chinese, Indonesian, Malay, Filipino and Southeast Asian populations**, so there is a real chance it appears on his report. It is not an illness and needs no treatment day to day, but it means his red blood cells break down when exposed to certain triggers.
>
> **If he is G6PD-deficient:**
> - **No mothballs (naphthalene).** This is the one to tell grandparents about explicitly — naphthalene mothballs in a wardrobe or stored bedding are a classic trigger, and they are still common in the region.
> - **No fava/broad beans** (and be careful with traditional/herbal preparations — some contain them).
> - **Certain medicines are off-limits** — some antimalarials, sulfa antibiotics, nitrofurantoin and others. **Tell every doctor, dentist and pharmacist, every time**, and don't accept a medicine without checking it first.
> - **Jaundice is the main newborn risk** — watch it more carefully, and take the Day 3–5 check seriously.
> - **Keep the result on your phone and in the paperwork folder.** It should be on his vaccination/health record card.
>
> It is X-linked, so it comes from the mother's side — worth knowing for any future children too.

## 4. Hearing screening

A quick, painless automated test (usually otoacoustic emissions, sometimes ABR) done while he is asleep, before discharge.

- **Result is "pass" or "refer".** A "refer" is common and usually means fluid or a restless baby, **not** deafness — it means the test should be repeated in a few weeks. Do the repeat.
- If a repeat still refers, ask for the referral immediately; early intervention for hearing is highly effective and every month counts.
- **Also watch at home:** he should startle or blink at sudden loud sounds, and settle or turn toward familiar voices from the first weeks.

## 5. Jaundice — the discharge check and the Day 3–5 follow-up

- Jaundice appears in **most** newborns from Day 2–4, peaking around Day 3–5. Frequent feeding is the main thing that helps it clear.
- The hospital will check a bilirubin level before discharge if he looks jaundiced, and may set up treatment (phototherapy) if it is high.
- **The Day 3–5 appointment (Thu 29 – Sat 31 Oct) is not optional.** It exists precisely because jaundice peaks *after* a well-baby discharge. Booked in [`pd-visits-and-vaccinations.md`](pd-visits-and-vaccinations.md).
- **Escalate immediately** if the yellow spreads to his legs/feet, he is too sleepy to wake for feeds, feeds poorly, or looks deeper yellow after Day 4 — full list in [`red-flags-and-emergencies.md`](red-flags-and-emergencies.md).

## 8. The first 72 hours at home

- **Days 2–4 are the hardest** — milk transition, cluster feeding, and the peak of the baby blues ([`postpartum-recovery.md`](postpartum-recovery.md) §6). Plan the support for these three days specifically.
- **Feed every 2–3 hours**, wake him if needed, and keep the feed/diaper log — it is the answer to every "is he okay?" question at the Day 3–5 visit.
- **Nobody but the parents sleeps in the same room as him on a bed or sofa** — the sofa rule is in [`newborn-care.md`](newborn-care.md). It is the single most common serious mistake of the first week.
- **Confirm the Day 3–5 appointment** on day one home. Do not let it drift into next week.
- **Anything from the red-flags list = call, don't wait**, and do not worry about being a nuisance: nobody at TMC or KKH is surprised by a first-time parent calling on day two.

- **CDA vaccination verification** for his Dependant's Pass needs confirmation of diphtheria and measles vaccination — it renews as the schedule progresses, so the birth-batch records are the start of that chain.
